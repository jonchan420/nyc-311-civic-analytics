# Recorded complaints are not the same as actual conditions

## What a complaint count measures
A 311 count measures how many people reported something, not how much of the problem exists. It mixes actual conditions with awareness of 311, access to a phone or the internet, language, trust in government, and willingness to report. A rise in recorded complaints can mean conditions got worse, more people started reporting, reporting got easier, or an agency changed how it records requests.

## Fewer complaints does not mean fewer problems
Some communities report less even when they face serious problems. This project's reporting-gap analysis compared housing complaints in 173 neighborhoods with what their housing conditions predict. Neighborhoods with more Chinese-speaking households with limited English filed fewer housing complaints than similar neighborhoods: about 34% fewer for every 10 percentage points more of those households. Chinatown/Lower East Side filed about 53% fewer housing complaints than its housing conditions predict. These are associations, not proof of what causes them.

## Why causes can't be read from counts
The data shows when and where complaints were recorded, not why. A spike that lines up with a storm is consistent with the storm causing it, but the counts alone don't establish that. Answers describe changes in recorded complaints and avoid claims about causes.

## Using counts for decisions
Allocating city resources by complaint counts alone would shortchange people who face the most barriers to reporting. Counts are best read alongside other evidence, such as inspections.
